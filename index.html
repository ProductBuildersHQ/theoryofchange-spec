    Impact Model¶

    Impact Model is an AI-native format from ProductBuildersHQ for describing how a program, product, or organization expects to bring about change. It is a causal model: it states which interventions should change which outcomes, why each step should lead to the next, and how to measure whether it did. It is a Go-first data model with generated JSON Schema, plus an SDK, the impact-model CLI, and an MCP server.

    You describe the change once, in one impact model. The problem tree, the theory of change, the logic model, the outcome map, the logframe, strategy formats such as OKRs and V2MOM, and improvement formats such as DMAIC are projections of it. Each draws the same model for a different reader and leaves something out, and none of them is a part of another.

    What is an impact model?¶

    An impact model is one structured description of how and why change is expected to happen. It holds:

    • the problem it responds to, the causes that produce it, and the effects it leads to;
    • the outcomes that should change, at five levels from output to impact, and the causal links between them, each with a reason to believe it;
    • the assumptions that must hold for a link to work, and the evidence for and against them;
    • what is done to bring the change about: interventions, and the resources and outputs they need and produce;
    • how progress is measured: indicators, targets, and observations over time;
    • who is accountable for each change, and who needs to see what.

    Every element records where it came from (stated, inferred, a hypothesis, or confirmed), so a reader can tell what an organization has claimed from what an assistant has proposed.

    The model is a graph of causes and measures. A diagram or a table of it is a projection, and one model can produce many.

    Projections¶

    Each projection answers a different question for a different reader:

    Projection Answers Get it with
    Problem tree What is the problem, what causes it, and what does it lead to? The core problem in the middle, causes (roots) below and effects (branches) above, with the outcomes that address each. See the guide report problem-tree, problem-tree-d2, problem-tree-json
    Theory of change Why should what we do lead to the change we want, and in what order must outcomes happen? Drawn as pathways of change, from the earliest outcome at the bottom to the long-term goal at the top report pathway
    Outcome map What changes, who is responsible for each change, and how is it measured? The outcomes by level, each with its sphere, owner, cause, and indicators report outcome-map
    Logic model What goes in, what is done, and what comes out? Stages from inputs, activities, and outputs through short-, medium-, and long-term outcomes to impact, one per pathway report logic-model, logic-model-d2
    Logframe What do we promise, and how will we verify it? Narrative, indicators with targets, means of verification, and assumptions, for contracts and monitoring report logframe
    Metric tree and headline metrics What does each program steer by, and what is each audience told? report metric-tree, report headline
    OKRs and outcome journeys What will a team commit to this quarter, and how does a person move through the outcomes? For the PRISM planning tools export okr, export journey
    V2MOM What do we want, what matters, how will we get it, what is in the way, and how will we know? Vision and values from the context, one method per pathway with its interventions as projects, measures from each pathway's North Star and guardrails, and obstacles from risks and open assumptions. See the mapping export v2mom
    DMAIC Which metrics are we improving, from what baseline to what target, what causes the gap, and is the gain holding? One category per pathway marked projections: [dmaic], its numeric indicators as metrics with observations as data points, control limits once there are enough, root causes from the problem tree, and interventions as improvement initiatives. Lint first checks that those pathways are measured often enough. See the mapping export dmaic

    Each projection leaves something out. A problem tree shows the problem, what causes it, and what it leads to, but not the steps by which a program changes it. A logic model shows the sequence from what goes in to what comes out, and rarely why each step holds. A theory of change shows why, and says less about what is delivered. A logframe fits the model into a matrix for contracts, and OKRs keep only what a team commits to for a period. None is derived from another. The impact model holds what they all need, so any of them can be drawn from it. A logic model that arrives on its own can seed a candidate impact model, but it cannot supply the reasons and assumptions it never held, so those stay marked as inferred until someone confirms them.

    impact-model report problem-tree impact-model.yaml      # the problem tree: causes, core problem, effects
impact-model report pathway impact-model.yaml           # the theory of change, as pathways of change
impact-model report outcome-map impact-model.yaml       # the outcome map
impact-model report logic-model impact-model.yaml       # a logic model for each pathway
impact-model report logframe impact-model.yaml          # the logframe matrix
impact-model report definitions impact-model.yaml       # all of them, plus metrics and glossary
impact-model export okr impact-model.yaml               # OKRs for the PRISM tools
impact-model export journey impact-model.yaml           # outcome journeys for the PRISM tools
impact-model export v2mom impact-model.yaml             # a V2MOM
impact-model export dmaic impact-model.yaml             # DMAIC improvement efforts

    Change the model once and every projection changes with it, so the numbers and labels never drift apart. (The outcome map here is the outcomes framework from the ActKnowledge method; it is not IDRC's Outcome Mapping, which tracks progress markers for the partners a program influences.)

    The theory of change¶

    The theory of change is the projection most programs start from. It explains how and why a program expects to bring about a change. It starts from the long-term goal and works backwards: what conditions must exist for the goal to be reached, what must exist before each of those, and so on down to what the program does today. Each step is an outcome; each arrow between outcomes is a causal link with a reason for believing it holds; and each belief that has to be true for a link to work is an assumption that can be tested.

    flowchart BT
    A[Staff write strong applications] -->|skilled staff apply more often| C[Members win more funding]
    B[Members share a funder list] -->|shared leads widen the pool| C
    C -->|funded work reaches more residents| D[Residents are better served]
    classDef ceiling stroke-dasharray: 4 3
    class D ceiling

    An illustration only. The dashed outcome sits above the organization's ceiling of accountability: the program tracks it and contributes to it, but does not claim it.

    Why it matters¶

    The method is used by foundations, development agencies, and funders, and the sources agree on five reasons:

    • Planning. Agree on what has to change, and in what order, before spending money on activities.
    • Closing the "missing middle". Show how day-to-day activities connect to a long-term goal, instead of asserting that they do.
    • Evaluability. Indicators and tests come from the causal reasoning, so you know what to measure and what would count as being wrong.
    • Communication. Funders, partners, staff, and the people the program serves can see the same reasoning and argue with it.
    • Learning. The model is a living framework. When evidence arrives, you revise it instead of defending it.

    How the chain, the metrics, and what each audience sees fit together¶

    An impact model is the chain from resources to impact. Metrics hang off each step of it, and different readers should see a different few. The first diagram is one program, shown as a pathway. The second shows how its metrics reach each audience.

    Reading the first diagram:

    • Resource and activity metrics show what went in and what was done. Output metrics show reach. Outcome metrics are the drivers: they say whether the change the program is for is happening.
    • A North Star is the one number that best captures the value a program delivers to the people it serves. It sits on the highest outcome the program answers for, at or below the ceiling of accountability, and it is an outcome, never a count of outputs. Each program can have its own, because programs deliver different value. Guardrails keep it honest by watching what it could hurt, such as equity, quality, or staff burden.
    • Impact sits above the ceiling. It is tracked and contributed to, not claimed.

    Reading the second diagram:

    • Every number is defined once, in one metric library. Two audiences that see the same figure see the same figure, with the same caveats and the same marker for whether it is attributable to the work or a contribution.
    • Headline metrics are what you tell people. The North Star is what you steer by. They can differ. The board, funders, members, government, and the community each get a short list, worded for them, drawn from the same indicators. A North Star may lead one list and not appear in another.
    • Every box is something you can record and report today. Mark an indicator as a program's North Star, with its guardrails, in the pathway's focus. Give each audience an ordered, worded headline list. impact-model report metric-tree shows a program's North Star and every indicator from activity to impact, and impact-model report headline shows what each audience is told. The amber boxes are the choices you make: which number a program steers by, and which few each audience leads with. impact-model lint checks them.

    What this project gives you¶

    Benefit How
    A model you can trust Every element records whether a source stated it, you inferred it, it is still a hypothesis, or reviewers confirmed it. Reports show how much of the model is established and how much is our reading
    Edits that cannot make it worse One validated, atomic patch engine behind the CLI, the SDK, and the AI tools. A change that adds structural errors is rejected, and --dry-run shows the result first
    Quality you can check A schema, structural validation, 72 deterministic lint rules, and a rubric for human or AI judges, so "is this a good impact model?" has an answer you can repeat
    Progress you can measure Load a spreadsheet of numbers and see which indicators, outcomes, and pathways are on track, with assumptions that can cap a result and a confidence score for each route to impact
    Artifacts for each audience Executive and funder reports, audience stories, slide decks, Word and PowerPoint files, CSV tables, and OKR, V2MOM, and DMAIC exports, all from one source and stamped with the version and hash
    Review that comes back Send a packet or sheet to reviewers, import their answers as proposed changes you inspect, then record approval
    Honest handling of people's data Stories and quotes appear only with consent, and restricted evidence is never rendered anywhere
    Ready for AI agents An MCP server and agent skills let an agent draft, edit, measure, report, and export, under the same rules as a person

    It is meant for program and strategy teams, evaluators, funders, and anyone who has to explain why what they do should lead to what they hope for, and then show whether it did.
